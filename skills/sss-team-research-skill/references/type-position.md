# Position papers

A position paper argues for one stance that a reasonable expert could disagree with, and supports it with evidence and reasoning rather than new experiments. ML venues (for example the ICML position track) also expect a fair treatment of opposing views.

## Section skeleton (for about 8 pages of main text)

| Section | Budget | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Title | | States the stance ("X should Y", "Why X needs Y"), not just the topic |
| Abstract | 150-250 words | Problem and why now (2 sentences), then one marked stance sentence ("We argue that..."), then the named concept or proposal, then what supports it, then the call to action in one clause |
| 1 Introduction | 0.75-1 page | Stake (with a number), status quo, the tension, the thesis in one sentence by paragraph 2-3, then 3-4 numbered contributions that each point to a section |
| 2 Background and definitions | 0.5-1 page | Fix the key terms and the scope, say what the position is *not*, and give quantified stakes plus one well-known real case |
| 3 The argument | 1.5-2 pages | Numbered premises leading to the conclusion. Give each premise evidence and tag it: empirical, theoretical, precedent or illustrative. A named concept with 3-5 parts and one diagram helps readers remember it |
| 4 Case or worked example | 1-1.5 pages | One concrete case (real or worked through) showing that the position changes a decision |
| 5 Alternative views | 1-1.5 pages | The 2-4 strongest opposing views, each stated so that its holders would accept the wording. For each: concede part, rebut part, and say what evidence would change your mind |
| 6 Recommendations / call to action | 0.5-1 page | A table of actor, action, trigger or evidence, and how to check it worked. Phrase the rule with an explicit exception ("X by default, unless Y") |
| 7 Conclusion | 0.3 page | Restate the stance, give the scope limits (conceptual, untested) and 3-4 concrete follow-ups |
| Impact statement | short | Who gains and who loses if the position is adopted |

## Rules

- **Falsifiable stance.** Write the sentence "This position is wrong if ___". If you cannot fill the blank with an observation or experiment, narrow the claim.
- **One claim per paper.** A framework or taxonomy supports the claim. It is not a second claim.
- **Small structures.** Use one organising structure of 3-5 items. Several nested lists (3 goals, 6 pillars, 5 gates, 4 enablers) are hard to remember and tend to overlap.
- **Steelman the opposition.** Address the strongest objection to the central claim, not only objections to your preferred mechanism. Include the costs of your own recommendation.
- **Label illustrative data** in captions. A hypothetical chart in an evidence section weakens the paper, so use real data where you can.
- **Concede, then restate.** A short "limits of our position" subsection before the final stance makes the stance more credible.
- **Calls to action name an actor and a verb.** Avoid "stakeholders should consider".

## Abstract formula

> [Problem, 1-2 sentences with a number]. [Why current practice or consensus falls short]. **We argue that [stance].** We [introduce/define] [named concept], [what it consists of]. Drawing on [evidence types], we show [key supporting point]. We [address/respond to] [main counterargument] and propose [concrete recommendation] for [actors].

## Figures

1. Concept figure: the position in one picture (pillars-to-goal, hub and spoke).
2. Evidence figure or table: real data or a comparison matrix with a "main risk" row for each option.
3. Action figure: a decision flow or gated process showing what happens when a check fails.

## Pre-submission checks

1. Do the title and abstract state a stance that a reviewer could oppose?
2. Does an alternative-views section exist, without a straw man?
3. Does every recommendation trace back to a premise, and does every premise have tagged evidence?
4. Is the "wrong if" sentence present?
5. Do the contributions match the sections without inflation?
6. Does the abstract claim nothing more than the body supports?
