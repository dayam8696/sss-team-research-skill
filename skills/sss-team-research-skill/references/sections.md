# Section recipes (any paper type)

Use these recipes together with the paper-type file. Where the type file is more specific, the type file wins.

## Title

- 10-15 words. Name the object, the method or approach, and the finding or scope.
- Two styles work well: a descriptive title ("Auditing Gender Bias in Text-to-Audio Generation Models") or a claim title ("Model Merging Undoes Concept Erasure in Diffusion Models"). A question title works for comparisons ("Are Foundation Models the Next-Generation Content Moderators?").
- Avoid "A Novel...", "Towards..." (unless it is a position paper), and acronyms that are not widely known.
- Offer 3 options to the user: descriptive, claim and question.

## Abstract (150-250 words, one paragraph)

Context (1 sentence), gap (1), what we did with scale (1-2), headline results with numbers (2), implication (1), scope or availability (1). Do not cite references in the abstract, and write out acronyms in full.

## Keywords

4-6 terms that are not already in the title. Mix broad and specific terms.

## Introduction

Use the funnel: the broad problem, the specific problem, the gap, our approach, RQs, contributions, roadmap. Use about 4-6 paragraphs. Each paragraph does one job (see writing-core).

## Related work / literature review

- Organise by 2-4 themes, not by paper.
- For each theme: what the theme covers, representative works (one clause each), their limitation **as it relates to our problem**, and how we differ.
- End with a summary sentence or a comparison table (prior work × attributes, with ● ◐ ○).

## Methodology

- Open with the overview figure and one sentence per stage.
- Write it so that a competent reader could repeat the study. Use the checklist in the type file.
- Use subsections that match the figure stages, in the same order and with the same names.

## Results

- Report, do not interpret (interpretation goes in the Discussion, except in merged "Results and Discussion" sections).
- Lead with the main table or figure. Follow the order of the RQs.
- Give every number with its unit, n and variance.

## Discussion

- Start with the answer to each RQ in one sentence each.
- Then interpretation, comparison with prior work, implications (practical and theoretical), and surprising results.
- Do not repeat the Results numbers. Refer to them.

## Limitations

- List 3-5 specific limitations. For each one, say how it limits the claim and what would fix it.
- Use a separate heading. Reviewers look for it.

## Conclusion (100-300 words)

- One sentence on what was done, 2-4 key findings (with numbers for empirical work), the contribution in one sentence, and 1-2 concrete future directions.
- Add no new results or citations. End on a precise statement.

## Future work / research agenda

- Each direction comes from a finding or gap in the paper. Write it as "Direction: ... Why: (evidence from our results) ... How: (study design)".

## Declarations (as the venue requires)

Data availability, code availability, funding, conflicts of interest, ethics approval, author contributions (CRediT), and the use of AI tools in writing.

## Response to reviewers (if asked)

Thank the reviewer once at the top, not in every reply. For each comment: quote it, then give the response, then the change made with its location ("Section 3.2, p. 5"). If you disagree, say so politely and give evidence.
